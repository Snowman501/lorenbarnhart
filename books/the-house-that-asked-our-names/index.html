The House That Asked Our Names

It asks one question: “What is your name?” Answer carefully.

Loren and Stacy Barnhart — married twenty-seven years — have just moved into an ordinary farmhouse outside South Solon, Ohio. The house stands over the buried remains of the 1811 Bell Meeting House. Something beneath it has kept its manners for two hundred years: it stops at the end of the driveway, it shows itself only at the edge of reflections — and it asks, politely, for names. In this house, a name given is a door opened.

An ordinary house

There is no mansion on a hill in this story, no castle, no gothic ruin. Just a two-story farmhouse with white siding, a deep porch, and a black rotary telephone in the front room that rings even though it was never plugged in — a telephone that was not there at the final walkthrough. What is under the house is older than the farm, older than the town, older than the meeting house whose buried stones it wears like a borrowed name.

The couple at the center of this novel are the author and his wife of twenty-seven years, written that way on purpose. The house is fiction. The marriage is not.
