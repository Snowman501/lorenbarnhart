The House That Asked Our Names

A South Solon Novel · Book One

Two hundred years apart, the same question. It begins in 1811.

Prologue

1811

The bell woke the child.

DONG.

She opened her eyes in the dark. Smoke scratched her throat. Pine pitch, and something sweeter beneath it.

Burning hair.

She sat up. "Mama?"

No answer. Orange light moved beneath the little room's door. Outside, a woman screamed. Then another. Feet hammered across the meeting-house floor. Men shouted. Somewhere overhead, wood cracked.

The child slid off the cot. The floorboards were warm under her bare feet. "Mama?"

The bell sounded again.

DONG.

Every voice outside stopped at once.

The child stared at the door. Smoke curled beneath it in thin gray fingers. A shadow crossed the strip of firelight. Boots stopped outside. The latch lifted.

Reverend Josiah Bell stood in the doorway.

Fire filled the aisle behind him. Men shoved at the main doors. Women clutched their children. The doors would not open.

A woman grabbed Bell's coat. "Josiah."

He looked down at her. Her hair had come loose. One side of her dress was burning.

"Please."

He touched her cheek. "It's all going to be all right."

She began to sob. A beam cracked above the congregation.

"Reverend!"

"Open the doors!"

"My boy!"

"Josiah!"

Bell stepped into the little room, and the woman behind him disappeared into the smoke. The child backed up against the wall.

"Where's Mama?"

Bell closed the door, and the screaming went muffled.

"Do you know why they're frightened?" he asked.

She shook her head.

He came closer. His black coat smelled of smoke. "They believe the fire can take something from them."

"I want Mama," the child said, crying now.

Bell crouched in front of her. Soot marked one side of his face. He did not look toward the door when someone called for help.

"What is your name?" he asked.

She hesitated. The meeting house groaned around them. Behind the door, someone began to pray. "Our Father, who art in Heaven—" More voices joined.

Bell waited. "What is your name?"

The child swallowed. "Rebecca."

The boards shifted under Rebecca's bare feet.

Bell repeated her name. "Rebecca."

"—hallowed be Thy name—"

Bell tilted his head. Listening. Not to the prayer. To whatever had moved beneath them. "Do you hear that?"

Rebecca shook her head.

Another crash. The walls trembled. Bell stood and opened the door, and fire rolled across the ceiling.

A man staggered out of the smoke and seized him by both shoulders. "Josiah! Help us!"

Bell stared at him. "You've called Him every Sunday of your life."

"What?"

"Call Him," Bell said, and then louder: "Call Him."

Someone shouted a prayer. Then another. The congregation took it up, voices colliding under the burning rafters. Rebecca stood in the doorway behind Bell and could barely breathe. Men shoved at the doors. Women screamed the names of their children. A little boy crawled under the benches.

Someone laughed.

Rebecca looked at Bell. The laughter was his. Quiet at first. Almost private. Then his shoulders began to shake, and the prayers faltered.

Bell lifted his face toward the burning ceiling. "He isn't coming."

The prayers broke off.

Bell lowered his eyes. "But I am here."

Something struck the underside of the floor. THUMP. Rebecca screamed.

The bell above them began to move. No one held the rope. It hung loose beside the pulpit. But up in the burning tower, the great brass bell swung once on its own.

DONG.

Everyone screamed.

Bell turned back toward Rebecca. Flames climbed the wall behind him. His shadow reached her feet.

"Don't be frightened," he said, and smiled.

"You answered."

The floorboards split.

Rebecca screamed.

Her scream rose above the cracking timber and the bell. Then it thinned into one steady note.

A dial tone.

Chapter One — The Arrival

Loren knew he had not picked up the telephone.

He had no memory of lifting the receiver.

Yet the receiver rested against his ear, and a steady dial tone filled it.

He stood motionless in the front room of the old farmhouse outside South Solon, Ohio, listening to a telephone that should not have worked. Late-afternoon sun came through the tall windows behind him. Cardboard boxes covered half the hardwood floor. Sweat had dried beneath his shirt, and his lower back hurt. Outside, Stacy was arguing with a folding table that had apparently decided it would rather die in the driveway than fit through the front door.

He lowered the heavy black receiver. The rotary phone sat on a narrow table beside the staircase.

He remembered setting a box of kitchen utensils on that table. He remembered bending down. Something had rung, and he'd thought Stacy had left her phone inside.

"Okay." His voice sounded too loud in the empty room. He set the receiver back in the cradle, and the dial tone stopped.

Outside, Stacy shouted. "Are you planning on helping me, or did you decide I should move us in by myself?"

"Coming."

"Today would be awesome."

"Love you too."

"Prove it."

Loren crouched beside the table.

He lifted the phone. No cord underneath. No wall jack behind the table. He ran a finger along the base, checking for a battery compartment.

He had heard it ring. He had heard a dial tone.

Stacy appeared in the doorway carrying one end of the folding table, her hair pulled back and already escaping around her face. "Baby? Are you gonna help me?"

"Yeah."

She followed his eyes. "What are you looking at?"

"The phone."

"The antique? I saw that at the final walkthrough."

Loren stared at her. "No, you didn't."

Her eyebrows rose. "I didn't?"

"That wasn't there."

Stacy gave him the look she reserved for an argument he would regret starting.

"Yes," she said. "It was."

"I would've noticed a telephone."

"You didn't notice the closet in the guest room until yesterday."

"That closet wasn't there either."

She smiled. "There was definitely a closet."

"I'm serious. You remember that exact phone? Right there?"

She shifted the table against her hip. "Loren. Help me before I murder this thing."

He took the other end. They wrestled the table through the doorway, and Stacy kissed his cheek before going back for another box.

The telephone sat in its cradle.

Maybe she was right. Maybe he'd picked it up on reflex when it rang. They had barely slept the night before, and moving could scramble anybody's head.
