From the shop to the page

Loren lives in Hot Springs Village, Arkansas. He spent more than twenty years as a dealer trained ASE Certified Master Technician, listening closely, following clues, and asking what everyone else might have missed. That habit of looking beneath the surface followed him into writing.

In his paranormal fiction, an ordinary place can hide an impossible truth. In his memoirs, he writes about the quieter mysteries: how a person survives difficult years, finds connection, and begins to speak honestly about what happened.

Why I write

Some stories start with a question you cannot put down. Others start with something you have carried for years.

Mine starts with a question I carried so long it nearly carried me out of this world:

Do I matter? Does my life matter? And if it does — what can a man like me possibly do that would make a difference?

I asked it as a boy in a house where silence did the talking. I asked it in a hospital room where I felt my own name disappear. I asked it behind the wheel of a truck I built with my own two hands, on Highway 71, on the night of February 14, 1999 — the night I had decided the answer was no.

That night, something answered me first. A voice said, “No, my son. Turn the truck around.”

So I turned around — and that same night, at a little apartment in Smithville, a girl walked out and said one word to me: “Hi.” Her name is Stacy. The next day, our twenty-seven years began. We were married seven months later, and she has answered my question every single day since. Not with speeches. Just by staying. By loving an ordinary, broken, stubborn man like it was the easiest thing in the world to do.

I asked the question one more time, years later, on my knees on the floor the night I thought I was going to lose her — begging God to take anything of mine He wanted, everything I had and everything I was, if He would only let her stay. He did. She is still here. So am I.

So now let me ask you to do something. Stop for a minute. Put down whatever has been telling you that you don’t matter — because I know that voice, and I know it lies loudest at night.

I am not saying this as a man who had it figured out. I’m saying it as living proof. They told me a man with my problems could never be an author. I write anyway — despite the doubt, despite the criticism, despite it all. And I use every tool set in front of me, yes, including AI, the way tools were meant to be used: out in the open, to build something honest. The stories are mine. The life is mine. I just won’t let the hard parts of how my brain works decide whether they get told.

Sylvester Stallone was told no, too. Broke, turned away, holding a script nobody wanted unless he stepped aside and let someone else live it. He bet on himself instead. Look where he ended up. I’m doing the same thing — with a pen.

I write because the strange and the deeply human belong on the same page. A haunting can ask what it means to remain yourself. A life story can ask what it takes to keep going, to love someone, and to choose yourself when you need to.

Whether you came for a mystery or a memoir, I hope you find a story that stays with you after the last page.

But more than that, I hope you hear this — from a man who almost wasn’t here to say it:

The struggle can only hold temporary. It has to break sometimes.

You matter. Your life matters. And it is not too late to turn around.

— Loren Barnhart
